Timeout values alone cannot establish service reliability because a timeout is a limit or a single observed duration, not an outcome. A request can exceed a timeout and still be retried successfully, or it can complete under the timeout while returning an error. The provided records are synthetic and show a regular arithmetic progression, so they do not demonstrate real failures, recovery, or user impact.

Reliability requires measurements of actual behavior over time. Needed signals include success and failure counts, error codes, availability or SLO attainment, request latency distributions such as p50, p95, and p99, throughput, saturation metrics like CPU, memory, queue depth, and connection pool usage, dependency latency, retry and circuit-breaker outcomes, and regional or service-level breakdowns. Logs, traces, and synthetic probes should be correlated with real-user monitoring to distinguish transient delays from persistent degradation. Capacity planning also needs load tests and trend analysis. Only by combining timeout behavior with outcome, performance, and resource measurements can an operator determine whether a service is reliable, where it is failing, and what remediation is required. These measurements should be collected continuously, compared against baselines, and reviewed during change events to separate configuration issues from infrastructure faults and capacity constraints over time.
