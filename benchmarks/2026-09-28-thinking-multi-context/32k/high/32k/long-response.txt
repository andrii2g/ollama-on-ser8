Timeout values alone cannot establish service reliability because a timeout is only a limit or a symptom, not proof that a request succeeded. A record may show a timeout_ms value without indicating whether the call completed, returned an error, was retried, or satisfied the user’s deadline. Reliability requires knowing the outcome of each request, not just the configured or observed time.

Additional measurements are needed: success and failure counts, error codes, HTTP or RPC status, retry outcomes, and whether retries masked failures. Latency distributions, especially p50, p95, and p99, show tail behavior. Availability, error budget burn rate, and SLO attainment connect performance to business impact. Throughput, saturation, queue depth, resource utilization, and dependency health reveal whether the service can sustain load. Logs, metrics, and traces should be correlated to distinguish timeouts from application errors, network issues, or downstream failures. In synthetic reference data, timeout_ms may simply follow a generated pattern, so it should not be interpreted as evidence of production health. A reliable assessment also needs context: service level objectives, expected traffic, regional behavior, and whether timeouts are caused by client limits, server processing, or network conditions. Together, these signals provide a defensible, evidence-based, and practical overall view of reliability.
